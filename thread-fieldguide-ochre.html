01

The palette, with one adjustment

Ochre is a light color, so it can't do everything red was doing. At full strength on paper it's a 2.9:1 contrast ratio — fine as a fill behind dark text, too weak for small text or thin lines. So it becomes a three-step ramp: ochre fills, a deeper ochre for lines, a deeper one still for text. Same hue family throughout, so it reads as one color.

Ink#1C2118All body text, headlines
ʻAlaea Ochre#C8873FFills: buttons, hub blocks, highlights
Ochre Line#AE7126Diagram strokes, rules, icons
Koa#9A6420Links, small labels, captions
Rule#E0DCD0Hairlines, dividers, borders
Paper#FCFBF7Every background

Why this matters

Buttons flip from white-on-color to ink on ochre — dark text on a warm gold field. That's 5.5:1, fully accessible, and it looks more expensive than white-on-red did. It's the single biggest visual improvement from the swap.

Every combination in this file passes WCAG AA. Nothing here will fail an accessibility audit, which matters when you're selling software to businesses that have to care about that.

What I'd do

Use two of them, in this order. These aren't really competing — they answer different questions, and a homepage needs both answers.

The Tangle goes at the top. It sells the problem in half a second and it's the only one that works on someone who arrived cold. The Specimen Plate goes one scroll down, once they're interested and want to see what they'd actually receive.

The Weave becomes the brand mark rather than a hero — favicon, section dividers, loading state, the graphic on your proposal cover. It's too good an ownership device to spend on a single spot, and too abstract to carry the top of the page alone.

If you want only one: The Tangle. It does the most work for the least explanation.
