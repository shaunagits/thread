# Thread — strategy review & content plan

Response to `Thread_Hawaii_Master_Strategy_Brand_Guide.docx` (v1.0, Aug 2026).
Written against the live site at https://threadhawaii.com.

Everything marked **DECIDE** needs an owner call before it can be built.

---

## 1. Viability verdict

**The concept is viable. Call it 7.5/10.** It's viable as a *practice* — one
person plus contractors, 6–12 real engagements a year, $250–500k revenue — not as
a studio that scales past that without a fundamentally different plan. Nothing in
the doc is wrong enough to sink it; the misallocation is in where effort goes
first.

### What's genuinely strong

**The name does work the brand doesn't have to.** "Thread" describes the actual
outcome — separate things becoming connected — rather than being an arbitrary
label. That's rare and it's worth protecting. Most agency names are decoration;
this one is an argument.

**The category choice is right.** "Custom software" over "app development" is
correct — broader, higher ticket, and it doesn't strand you when the honest
answer is an integration rather than an app. The doc's willingness to say
*"custom software is not automatically the answer"* is the single most
trust-building line in it.

**Hawaii is a real moat, for a boring reason.** Not culture — proximity and
referral density. B2B in Hawaii runs on relationships, the local competitive set
is thin, and a named person who answers the phone beats a mainland agency that
assigns an account manager. Every closed deal makes the next one easier in a way
it wouldn't in Austin.

**"If I Owned…" is the best idea in the document.** It solves the cold-start
problem. You have no case studies; this manufactures proof of *judgment* while
you wait for proof of *delivery*. It's also the only asset in the plan that
compounds — episodes stay useful for years, feed SEO, feed LinkedIn, and become
the industry pages the doc wants later. Treat it as infrastructure, not content.

### What I'd push back on

**1. The differentiation is currently a claim, not a proof.**
"We understand your business first" is what every consultancy on earth says. It
can't be asserted — it has to be demonstrated before the prospect talks to you.
Two things demonstrate it: the "If I Owned" series and the system map. Everything
else is decoration. Budget accordingly.

**2. SEO is weighted as an acquisition channel. It's a credibility channel.**
"Custom software Hawaii" and its siblings are plausibly 10–50 searches/month
combined. Even at a great conversion rate that's a handful of inquiries a year.
That's not a reason to skip it — the intent is excellent and the pages cost
little — but if the 90-day plan treats SEO as the pipeline, the pipeline will be
empty at day 90. **Year one pipeline is referral + LinkedIn + direct outreach.**
SEO is what makes you look real when someone Googles you after a referral, which
is its own high-value job.

**3. Six capabilities reads as "we do everything," which undercuts the pitch.**
Apps + business software + automation + integrations + AI + MVP is a menu, and a
menu contradicts "we start with your problem." Automation and integrations are
the same sale. And MVP/startup product work is a *different business* — different
buyer, longer sales cycle, worse payment behaviour, higher failure rate. It
dilutes the operations positioning that's actually working. See §3.

**4. There are two brand voices in play and the doc's is the weaker one.**
The site says *"So a person becomes the integration."* The doc says *"Software
built around the way your business actually works."* The first is a sentence
someone repeats to their business partner. The second is landing-page wallpaper.
The doc's *rules* are right (start with the problem, plain language, no hype) —
but its example copy lands in the safe generic zone it warns against.
**Keep the site's voice, use the doc's strategy.** DECIDE.

**5. "I" or "we"?** The doc's elevator pitch says "I build custom software." The
site says "we." Pick one. For this market I'd argue **"I," with a name and a
face** — the whole advantage is being a person rather than a firm, and Hawaii
buyers will find out you're solo in the first ten minutes anyway. Leading with it
converts better than being caught out by it. DECIDE.

**6. The credibility gap is the actual bottleneck, and right now it's worse than
empty.** §04 and §05 of the live site contain invented products, invented pricing,
and a fabricated Kailua case study with fabricated metrics. A prospect who reads
that specimen and asks "great, can I call them?" produces a conversation that
ends the deal and the referral chain behind it. In a market this small, one of
those is expensive. §4 below replaces both sections with things that are real on
day one and require nobody's permission.

**7. "If I Owned…" carries a reputational landmine in a market this small.**
The doc's framework says research 5–10 real businesses and map their friction.
Publishing "here's what's broken about how this tour company operates" — even
politely, even unnamed-but-identifiable — in a community where everyone knows
everyone is a bad trade. **Never name the businesses. Present a composite.** Say
so explicitly in the episode: *"This is a composite of eight real operations, not
any one company."* It's more useful anyway, and it protects the referral network
you're trying to build.

### The one structural change I'd make

**Productize the front door.** Right now the site's primary CTA is "Get your
systems mapped," and the map is described as step 01 of a process. Stop treating
it as a step. Make it **the product** — named, priced, scoped, with its own
section and eventually its own page.

Why this matters more than anything else in this document:

- "Start a Project" asks a stranger for a $40k decision. "Get your systems
  mapped" asks for a week of access and hands back an artifact they keep.
- It's the *only* offer where your differentiator (business understanding) is the
  deliverable rather than a promise about the deliverable.
- It qualifies brutally. Someone who won't give you a week of access will not
  survive a twelve-week build.
- It generates real case-study material before you have case studies. A map is
  publishable (anonymised) even if they never hire you.
- It converts the sales process from persuasion into work. You stop selling and
  start showing them their own business.

**DECIDE: free or paid?** The site currently says free. I'd charge **$2,500,
credited in full against the build**. Free discovery attracts tire-kickers and
quietly prices your thinking at zero — which is a problem when your thinking is
the product. Paid-and-credited costs the serious buyer nothing and filters
everyone else. Keep a free version in your pocket for warm referrals.

## 3. Services & offer architecture

Collapse the doc's six capabilities into **four services, one entry product, one
back end.** Lead every one with the business outcome; the technology goes in the
meta column where it reassures without leading.

### 0. The Map — *entry product*

> One week. I trace how work actually moves through your business — every tool,
> every handoff, every place a person is doing the job an integration should do —
> and hand you a drawn map of it with a ranked list of what's worth fixing first.
> Yours to keep, whether or not you hire me.

- **Price:** $2,500, credited in full against any build started within 90 days
- **Deliverable:** the map (printable), a friction inventory, a ranked build list
  with rough costs, and a written recommendation — *including "do nothing" or
  "buy this $50/mo product instead" when that's the honest answer*
- **Why it exists:** qualification, proof of judgment, case-study raw material

### 1. Operations dashboards — *from $12,000*

One screen that answers the questions you currently open four tabs to answer.
Live data from every system you run, in the shape your team thinks in.
*Typical 3–6 weeks.*

### 2. Custom internal apps — *from $25,000*

The software your business needs and nobody sells: job tracking shaped like your
workflow, intake that routes itself, approvals that match how decisions actually
get made. *Typical 6–10 weeks.*

### 3. Automation & integration — *from $6,000*

Connect the tools you already pay for and delete the retyping. The unglamorous
half — making systems agree, so the same number means the same thing everywhere.
*Typical 2–6 weeks.*

### 4. Practical AI — *from $8,000*

AI where it holds up and nowhere else: pulling data out of documents and forms,
routing inbound work, drafting the repetitive correspondence, flagging what needs
a human. Human review built in. *Typical 4–8 weeks.*

### 5. Care plan — *from $750/month* — **the back end nobody planned for**

Neither document treats support as a service. It should be: it's the margin, it's
the stability, and it's how a project becomes a relationship. Hosting, monitoring,
small changes, a standing monthly hour. Offer it in the proposal, not after
launch — attach rate collapses once the project ends.

**Cut from the front:** MVP / startup product development. Different buyer,
different economics, different risk. Keep it as one line on the contact form
("I occasionally take on MVP work for founders — ask") so you don't turn away a
good one, but keep it off the navigation. It contradicts the operations
positioning that's doing the work.

**Pricing note:** the numbers above are a coherent *starting ladder*, not
research. Sanity-check them against what you'd actually accept before they go
live. What matters structurally is that a floor appears on the services section —
"From $X" filters out the $3k website buyer without you having to have that
conversation nine times.

## 4. Homepage plan

The current structure is good and the writing in §01–§02 is the best asset in the
project. The fix is surgical: **§04 and §05 are the fabricated sections, and both
should be replaced by things that are real on day one and require no client's
permission.**

| § | Now | Becomes |
|---|---|---|
| Hero | Field guide | **Keep**, tighten CTA |
| — | Connects strip | **Keep**, prune to tools you've actually built against |
| 01 | The problem | **Keep verbatim** |
| 02 | How it works | **Keep**, adjust step 01 if the map goes paid |
| 03 | Services | **Revise** — four services, price floors in meta |
| 04 | Products *(invented)* | **The Map** — a real, sellable offer you control |
| 05 | Work *(fabricated)* | **If I Owned…** — real thinking, no client needed |
| 06 | About | **Keep**, add name and face |
| 07 | Contact | **Replace** generic form with a qualifying intake |

That swap solves the live-fabrication problem without waiting on a client to give
permission for a case study. It also improves the page: a named entry offer and
published thinking are better proof than three invented SKUs.

### Draft copy

#### Hero — keep, one change

Headline, lead and figure all stay. Change the button pair to make the map the
obvious first step and give the second button somewhere real to go:

> **[ Get your systems mapped — $2,500 ]**  **[ See how it works ]**

Margin note, revised for paid:

> Every engagement opens with a system map — yours, drawn from your actual stack,
> before a line of code is written. It takes about a week, it costs $2,500, and
> the full amount comes off the build. If the map says you don't need me, you
> keep the map and we shake hands.

That last sentence is the whole brand in one line. Use it.

#### §03 — Services

Heading stays: *"Four kinds of work, one underlying job."*
Add the floor to each service's meta column (`From $12,000` · `Typical 3–6 weeks`
· `You own the code`). Note text:

> Fixed-scope projects at a fixed price, or a monthly care plan for ongoing build
> and support. No hourly billing, no change-order games. Most engagements are a
> combination — the map tells us which.

#### §04 — The Map *(replaces Products)*

> **§ 04 — The Map**
> ### The first thing I build is a drawing.
>
> Before anyone talks about software, I spend a week inside how your business
> actually runs. Not the org chart — the real path a job takes from the phone
> ringing to the money landing, and every point where a person is doing work a
> system should be doing.
>
> You get a drawn map of it. A list of every place time leaks. And a ranked set
> of what's worth building, with rough numbers, starting with the smallest thing
> that would make a real difference.
>
> It's $2,500 and it comes off the build in full. Sometimes the map says the
> answer is a $50-a-month product you haven't heard of, or a change to how
> Tuesday works. I'll tell you that. You keep the map either way.
>
> *In the margin:* Roughly a week. Two conversations, a look at your systems, and
> a half-day with whoever actually does the work — that last one matters most.
> Owners describe the process they designed; the map records the one that exists.

#### §05 — If I Owned… *(replaces Work)*

> **§ 05 — Field notes**
> ### I do the homework before you hire me.
>
> A running series where I take a kind of Hawaiʻi business, study how it really
> operates, map where the work leaks, and design what I'd build first if it were
> mine. No client, no invoice — just the thinking, in public, so you can judge it
> before you pay for it.
>
> Every episode is a composite drawn from several real operations. Nobody is
> named.
>
> — If I Owned a Hawaiʻi Tour Company
> — If I Owned a Hawaiʻi Contractor
> — If I Owned a Hawaiʻi Property Management Company
>
> *In the margin:* When real client work is publishable, it'll appear here beside
> the field notes, with permission and with the client's numbers rather than
> mine.

That last note is important — it's an honest placeholder that reads as
deliberate restraint instead of an empty section.

#### §06 — About

Keep the three paragraphs; they're good. Add a name, a photo, and one line of
provenance ("Fifteen years building X before Y" — whatever's true). Right now the
page has no human in it, which is odd for a brand whose entire pitch is *you get
a person, not an account manager.*

#### §07 — Contact

Replace name/email/message with a short qualifying intake. Five fields, not
eight — the doc's eight-question list is a discovery-call agenda, not a form:

1. What's not working today? *(long text — the only required narrative field)*
2. What would you like to happen instead?
3. What systems are you running? *(free text)*
4. Rough budget — `Under $10k` / `$10–25k` / `$25–60k` / `$60k+` / `Not sure yet`
5. Name, business, email, phone

Then: *"I read every one of these myself and reply within one business day. If
it's not a fit, I'll say so and point you somewhere better."*

The budget selector is the highest-value field on the page. It's not rude — it's
the fastest way to avoid wasting a stranger's afternoon.

## 5. Customer journey & pipeline

The doc's flow — *Inquiry → Discovery → Workflow analysis → Recommendation →
Scope → Proposal → Build → Launch → Support* — describes the **delivery** process
well and the **acquisition** process not at all. Everything upstream of "Inquiry"
is missing, and that's where the work is.

### The journey

| Stage | What they're thinking | What has to exist | What moves them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Unaware** | "This is just how it is." | — | Nothing. Not your buyer yet. |
| **1. Nagging** | "We waste so much time on this." | LinkedIn posts, "This Should Be Software" | Recognition — naming their problem better than they can |
| **2. Looking** | "Is there something for this?" | Search pages, GBP, an If I Owned episode for their industry | Proof of judgment |
| **3. Interested** | "Could this person help *us*?" | Homepage §01, field notes, About with a face | Feeling understood before contact |
| **4. Ready to talk** | "What would this cost and what happens first?" | Price floors, The Map section, intake form | A small first step that isn't a $40k commitment |
| **5. Inquiry** | "Sent it. Hope they're real." | Intake form, fast reply | Reply speed. Under 24 hours, always. |
| **6. Fit call** | "Do they get it?" | 25 minutes, structured | You asking better questions than they expected |
| **7. The Map** | "This is genuinely useful already." | The map engagement | The artifact |
| **8. Proposal** | "Which version do we do?" | Three options, fixed prices | Choice of *scope*, not choice of *whether* |
| **9. Build** | "Is this on track?" | Working software every two weeks | Visible progress, not status reports |
| **10. Handover** | "Do we understand what we own?" | The documentation plate | Ownership without dependency |
| **11. Care plan** | "Who do we call?" | Retainer offered *in the proposal* | Continuity |
| **12. Advocate** | "You should talk to my guy." | A referral ask on the calendar | Being asked, at the right moment |

**Stage 4 is the gap in both documents.** Between "reading the site" and
"contacting a stranger about a five-figure project" there is nothing. The Map
fills it. So do the doc's parking-lot lead magnets — of those, the **App Cost
Calculator** is the best (it answers the question everyone is actually
Googling), and I'd build it as page two of the website, not someday.

### The pipeline

Eight stages. Track in anything — a spreadsheet is genuinely fine at this volume;
a CRM is a solved problem you don't have yet.

| Stage | Exit criteria | Target time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Inquiry | Form received | — |
| 2. Qualified | Real problem, real budget band, decision-maker involved | 1 day |
| 3. Fit call held | 25 min, problem + budget + timeline + authority confirmed | 5 days |
| 4. Map sold | Deposit paid, kickoff scheduled | 14 days |
| 5. Map delivered | Map + ranked build list handed over | 21 days |
| 6. Proposal sent | Three options, fixed prices, care plan included | 25 days |
| 7. Won / lost | Signed, deposit paid | 40 days |
| 8. Delivered | Launched, documented, care plan offered | +6–12 wks |

**Planning assumptions** (guesses to replace with real numbers after 10 inquiries
— do not treat as data): inquiry → qualified 60%; qualified → fit call 70%; fit
call → map 35%; map → build **65%+**. That last number is the point of the whole
structure: by the time someone has paid for a map and watched you understand
their business, the build is the obvious next step rather than a decision.

**Disqualify fast and say so kindly.** Budget under $10k. Wants a marketing
website. Has an app idea but no business and no money. Wants a technical
cofounder. Equity instead of cash. "Just send me a quote" with no willingness to
talk. Anyone whose decision-maker won't join a call. Sending a warm "not a fit,
here's who I'd call instead" costs one email and buys goodwill in a market where
that circulates.

**Referral ask, scheduled:** day 30 and day 90 after launch. Not "let me know if
you know anyone" — *"Who else do you know running six systems and reconciling
them by hand on Fridays?"* Specific asks get answers.

## 6. Suggestions, ranked by leverage

1. **Replace §04 and §05 this week.** Fabricated pricing and a fabricated client
   are live and indexable. §4 above gives you replacements that need no
   permissions and improve the page. This is the highest-value change available.
2. **Google Business Profile.** Buried at §10 in the doc; it's plausibly the
   single highest-ROI item in the whole plan. "Software developer Honolulu" local
   pack is winnable, it costs an afternoon, and it's the one channel where being
   physically in Hawaiʻi is a ranking factor rather than a story.
3. **Publish one "If I Owned" episode before touching any other content.** One
   finished episode is worth more than four service pages. It's the proof, the
   sales asset, the LinkedIn engine and the industry-page seed simultaneously.
   Pick contractors or tour companies — biggest local population, most obvious
   friction.
4. **Cut the SEO page count from four to three.** `/services`,
   `/custom-software-hawaii`, `/app-development-hawaii` and `/automation-ai-hawaii`
   will cannibalise each other at this site size. Do `/services` +
   `/custom-software-hawaii` + `/automation-ai-hawaii`, and fold app development
   into the custom-software page as a section.
5. **Build the App Cost Calculator as page two.** Highest-intent question in the
   market, nobody local answers it, and it captures people at stage 4 who aren't
   ready to talk yet.
6. **Get one real case study by giving away one map.** Offer a free map to a
   business you'd want as a logo, in exchange for permission to publish it
   anonymised. That's a week of work that buys the credibility asset you're
   otherwise blocked on for months.
7. **Write the care plan into every proposal from day one.** Retainer attach rate
   is near zero if you offer it after launch and high if it's line item three.
8. **Two LinkedIn posts a week, one of them visual.** The doc's mix (40%
   observations / 30% concepts / 20% educational / 10% BTS) is right. The one
   change: post the *concepts* as images. Screenshots of dashboards you designed
   for nobody outperform text on LinkedIn by a wide margin, and they demonstrate
   the thing you're claiming.
9. **Say "you don't need this" in public, twice a quarter.** "You Probably Don't
   Need AI" is the strongest post idea in the doc. Restraint is the most
   credible marketing available to a technology practice and almost nobody uses
   it.
10. **Name the map.** "The Map" works but it's generic in search and hard to
    refer to. Something ownable — *Thread Map*, *The Systems Map*, *Fig. 1* —
    makes it a thing people can recommend by name.

### Deliberately parked

- **Industry pages** — the doc is right to hold these until real evidence exists.
  The If I Owned episodes become them naturally.
- **YouTube** — correct as backlog. It's a second job.
- **Instagram** — keep, but as a mirror of LinkedIn's visual posts. Zero original
  effort until LinkedIn is consistent for a quarter.
- **Products (Thread Panel / Handoff / Field Kit)** — the productisation instinct
  is good and premature. Build the same thing four times for clients first, then
  productise what repeats. Until then it's inventory you can't support.

assumptions in §5 with real conversion rates and re-plan from there.
