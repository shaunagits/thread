Three ways Thread could look.

Same story in all three: Thread is the connecting line that pulls a business's scattered tools into one place. What changes is the register — how technical, how warm, how much of the island shows through.

HAWAII: ATMOSPHERIC, NOT LITERAL AUDIENCE: LOCAL + MAINLAND, EQUAL TONE: WARM & HUMAN MUST CARRY: SERVICES + SAAS
01

Woven

The metaphor, made material

Takes the name literally and earns it. Kapa-cloth warmth, over-under structure, ochre and indigo pulled from natural dye. This is the direction where the logo actually means something and the brand has a story a client repeats back to you.

Typography

Fraunces — headlines. A serif with softness and wobble in it; reads handmade without reading fragile.

Work Sans — body & UI. Slightly humanist, holds up in dense dashboard screenshots.

Imagery rules

  • Macro texture: woven fiber, wood grain, hand-dyed cloth — abstracted, never a full lauhala hat
  • Product shots sit on linen, not white
  • People: hands and workspaces over posed headshots
  • Never: palm trees, hibiscus, sunsets, tiki anything

Where the thread lives

The over-under crossing becomes the system's connective device: section dividers, the line that runs between integration logos, the loading animation, the diagram style on service pages.

Wins

Most ownable of the three. Nobody in custom software looks like this, and the name finally pays off visually. Local clients feel the place; mainland clients read it as taste, not as a regional accent.

Watch out

Craft warmth can undercut technical credibility if the ochre runs loose. Needs disciplined, almost cold UI inside product screens so the SaaS side still reads as serious software.

Deep Water

Calm expertise, ocean depth

The place shows up as depth of color rather than as imagery. Ink-dark navy, a teal current, warm sand to keep it human, and a sunrise coral used once per page. Feels like a firm that has done this before and isn't nervous about your migration.

Typography

Instrument Serif — headlines only. High contrast, editorial, gives warmth to an otherwise engineered palette.

Inter — everything else. The default language of good software; buyers read it as competence.

Imagery rules

  • Real product UI is the hero image — dark panels, live data, no fake charts
  • Photography: water surface, depth gradients, horizon lines at dusk
  • Sunrise coral appears once per screen. Once.
  • Never: stock developers at laptops, glowing AI brains, circuit boards

Where the thread lives

A single teal line that enters at the top of the page and passes through every section — behind the integration grid, along the process steps, terminating at the CTA. Scroll-linked, so it draws as you go.

Wins

Best fit for selling SaaS at a high price. Dark UI makes product screenshots look expensive, and it competes head-on with mainland agencies without trying. Easiest of the three to execute consistently.

Watch out

Closest to the category default — dark-navy-plus-teal is well-trodden. Warmth has to come from the writing and the serif, or it drifts into generic B2B. Least distinctive if built carelessly.

03

Daylight

Editorial, open, unmistakably human

Light-first and almost print-like. Warm paper, deep kalo green, a lava terracotta accent. Hawaiʻi arrives as quality of light and upland botany rather than beach. This is the direction that most sounds like a person you'd want to hire.

Typography

Newsreader — headlines and pull quotes. Reads like a well-set magazine; invites reading rather than scanning.

Public Sans — body & UI. Plain, legible, slightly civic. Keeps the editorial feel from turning precious.

Imagery rules

  • Bright, high-key photography with real shadows — never flat studio lighting
  • Upland green: ferns, kalo, ʻōhiʻa, mist on a ridge. No beaches.
  • Product UI shown light-mode, framed in generous white space
  • Never: gradient blobs, 3D renders, dark hero sections

Where the thread lives

A hairline rule is the whole system — it underlines the logo, separates sections, connects steps in a process, and terminates in a lava dot wherever something is a destination or a CTA.

Wins

Highest trust per pixel, and the strongest fit for a warm-and-human brief. Excellent for long-form service pages, case studies, and a founder-led voice. Local buyers respond to it fastest.

Watch out

Light-mode makes premium SaaS harder to sell — product screenshots read cheaper on white than on dark. Needs a genuinely strong dark counterpart for the product side, which is extra design work.

What I'd do

Woven for the brand, Deep Water for the product. They share a spine — warm neutral paper, one deep blue, one earth accent — so they're not two brands, they're one brand at two temperatures.

Woven gives you the thing the other two can't buy: a mark and a story that are actually yours, tied directly to what the name means. That's what makes a first meeting easy and a referral repeatable. Marketing site, service pages, proposals, the deck — all Woven.

Then the SaaS products and any logged-in dashboard shift into Deep Water's dark palette, keeping the same indigo family and the same crossing motif as the loading and connection graphic. Dark UI is where high-priced software has to live, and it solves Woven's one real weakness.

Daylight is the one I'd hold. It's the warmest and the most trustworthy, but it fights you on the product half of the business, and you'd end up building two systems anyway.

To decide, answer these

  • Which half of the business pays the bills in year one — services or products? If services, Daylight climbs. If products, Deep Water climbs.
  • Do you want the brand to feel like you, or like a firm? Woven and Daylight say person. Deep Water says company.
  • Would a Waikīkī hotel GM and a Denver ops director both feel this was made for them? Test each direction against both.
