# Thread offer design, journey and pipeline

Companion to `HOMEPAGE-COPY.md`. This is the strategy behind the copy: how the
free map stays sustainable, what the service ladder is, how someone gets from
never having heard of Thread to signing, and how you work the pipeline.

Builds on `STRATEGY.md` rather than repeating it. Where I disagree with that
document, I say so at §7.

---

## 1. The strategic reframe

### Your market is small enough to name

This is the single most useful fact about Thread's position, and it changes what
the plan should be.

Hawaiʻi has 139,922 small businesses. But 115,475 of those have no employees at
all. The real employer base is **24,447 firms**, and the ones with enough
operational complexity to need custom software are the ones with 20 or more
people:

| Segment | Firms in Hawaiʻi |
|---|---|
| No employees | 115,475 |
| 1 to 19 employees | 21,713 |
| **20 to 499 employees** | **2,734** |

Inside that 2,734, the largest industries by firm count. Your brand guide's
priority sectors are spread across these rows: contractors sit in Construction,
tour and activity operators in Transportation and in Arts, Entertainment and
Recreation (81 firms), property management in Real Estate:

| Industry | Firms, 20 to 499 employees |
|---|---|
| Accommodation and food services | 540 |
| Health care and social assistance | 310 |
| Construction | 300 |
| Retail trade | 255 |
| Professional, scientific and technical | 228 |
| Other services | 223 |
| Administrative and support | 206 |
| Wholesale trade | 171 |
| Transportation and warehousing | 133 |
| Real estate, rental and leasing | 86 |

*Source: SBA Office of Advocacy, 2024 Hawaiʻi Small Business Profile (2021 Census
data). Add the upper end of the 1 to 19 band, roughly firms with 10 to 19 people,
and the realistic addressable set is somewhere around 4,000 to 6,000 firms.*

**What this means practically.** You need 6 to 12 engagements a year out of a
few thousand named companies. That is not a marketing funnel problem. It is a
list problem. You can literally build a spreadsheet of 200 target businesses,
by name, with an owner's name against each, in a week.

That list is worth more in year one than every SEO page combined. Build it
before you build anything else.

### The competitive threat is not other agencies

It's that in 2026 a capable owner or a junior person can generate a working
internal tool themselves in a weekend. That's real and it's accelerating.

So "Thread can build software" is not a moat. Two things are:

1. **Knowing what to build.** Most of these attempts fail on the definition,
   not the code. That is exactly what the map sells.
2. **Being here in year three.** The tool built in a weekend has no owner, no
   documentation, no monitoring and no one to call. That is what the care plan
   sells.

Both of your strongest offers, in other words, are strengthened rather than
threatened by this. But they need to be the ones you lead with, and neither one
is currently on the site.

### Rescue work is the wedge nobody planned for

Direct consequence of the above. There is a growing population of businesses
sitting on half-finished software: something a contractor built and abandoned,
an automation the departed ops manager set up, a tool the owner generated
themselves that mostly works.

Why it's a good wedge:

- **Urgency is already there.** Nobody shops around when the thing that runs
  their scheduling breaks.
- **Short sales cycle.** No one needs convincing that a problem exists.
- **It leads naturally to the care plan**, which is the recurring revenue.
- **It's a low-risk first engagement** that becomes a build relationship.

Put it on the homepage as a callout, and make it one of the three things you
mention in outbound.

### Buying is triggered, not gradual

Nobody wakes up wanting custom software. They buy after an event:

- Someone quit and took the process with them
- A system they depend on is being sunset or repriced
- A failed SaaS implementation
- An audit, a compliance requirement, or an insurance change
- Growth past a threshold, usually a second location or crossing ~25 staff
- A bad customer incident traceable to a dropped handoff
- A new GM or ops manager with a mandate to fix things

**This is what outbound and content should target.** Not general awareness.
"Congrats on the second location" is a better opener than anything about
software. New-hire announcements on LinkedIn, permit filings and new-location
news are all public.

## 2. The free systems map, designed to stay free

You want it free but bounded. Here is the design. Five constraints, one of which
is the automation.

### The five limits

**1. Scope: one workflow, not the whole business.**
> Lead to invoice. Job to payment. Intake to delivery. One process, start to
> finish.

This is the most important limit, and it's the one that makes the map genuinely
deliverable in hours rather than days. A whole-business map is a paid
engagement, and the free one is how someone finds out whether it's worth buying.

**2. Time: one conversation, ninety minutes, capped.**
Walked with the person who actually does the process, not just the person who
owns it. Not a series of meetings.

**3. Volume: two per month, stated publicly.**
Real scarcity, honestly described. Note this cap covers *requested* maps, the
full ninety-minute engagement. The speculative partial maps in §6 are a
different artifact: built from public information, an hour or two each, sent
unsolicited. They don't count against the two and the site shouldn't imply
otherwise. Two a month at three to four hours each is
eight hours a month, which is sustainable indefinitely. It also removes any need
for salesy urgency language, which would cut against the voice you picked.

**4. The intake form is the gate, and it does the work.**
This is the automation. A ten-minute structured form collects everything you'd
otherwise spend the first forty minutes of a call collecting, and it filters at
the same time. Someone who won't spend ten minutes on a form will not survive a
twelve-week build.

Route the responses:

| Signal | Response |
|---|---|
| Fits, real budget band, decision-maker involved | Book the ninety minutes |
| Fits, no budget yet, but real problem | Offer the map anyway if a slot is open. These become next year. |
| Off-the-shelf product solves it | Auto-reply naming the product. Costs nothing, buys real goodwill. |
| Under $10k, wants a website, no business behind the idea | Kind decline with a referral elsewhere |

That third row matters more than it looks. Your brand guide's best line is
*"Thread should be willing to recommend an existing SaaS product."* Automating
that reply means you actually do it, every time, instead of only when you have
the energy.

**5. The deliverable is templated.**
Same three parts every time:

- **Current state**, drawn. One diagram.
- **Friction inventory.** Where information is re-entered, where it waits, where
  it gets lost, where a person is the integration.
- **Ranked list of fixes**, each with rough effort, rough cost, and an honest
  "or don't" where that's the answer.

With a fixed template and the intake already filled in, the drafting is fast.
Budget three to four hours including the call.

### The part worth saying out loud

Thread's intake is automated, its discovery is productised, and its deliverable
is templated. That is precisely what Thread sells. Say so, once, somewhere:
*"The intake you just filled in is the kind of thing Thread builds."*

### When to start charging

You picked free, and free is right while you have no proof. Revisit when all
three of these are true:

1. Three maps delivered, and published anonymised with written permission
2. A redacted sample map downloadable from the site
3. More requests than slots, twice in a row

At that point the same offer is sellable at $1,500 to $2,500 credited against a
build, and the whole-business version prices above it. Nothing on the site has
to change today.

**Do build the sample.** Nobody buys, or even requests, an abstract deliverable.
A downloadable example map will do more for conversion than any copy on the page.

## 3. The service ladder

One free entry offer, four services, one recurring back end, one wedge.

| # | Offer | Price | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Systems map** | Free, capped | Qualification, proof of judgment, case study material |
| 1 | Automation and integration | from `[$X]` | Cheapest real build. Best first project. |
| 2 | Dashboards and reporting | from `[$X]` | Highest visible value per dollar |
| 3 | Practical AI | from `[$X]` | Modern, specific, sells itself when scoped honestly |
| 4 | Custom internal apps | from `[$X]` | The big one |
| 5 | **Care plan** | from `[$X]`/mo | The margin and the relationship |
| + | Rescue work | scoped case by case | High-urgency entry point |

**Cut from the front:** MVP and startup product development. Different buyer,
different economics, different risk profile, and it dilutes the operations
positioning. One line on the contact form, nothing in the navigation.

**On pricing.** Your brand guide correctly marks transparent pricing as
`RESEARCH`. I'm not going to invent numbers, and the site should not carry any
until you've decided them. But do put a floor on the services section before you
launch, even a rough one. "From $X" ends the $3,000-website conversation before
it starts, and you will otherwise have that conversation nine times.

Three questions that will settle your floors faster than research:

1. What is the smallest project you'd be pleased to take?
2. What is the smallest one you'd resent by week three?
3. Set the floor at the second number, not the first.

## 4. The customer journey

Your brand guide's flow (Inquiry → Discovery → Analysis → Recommendation → Scope
→ Proposal → Build → Launch → Support) describes **delivery** well and
**acquisition** not at all. Everything before "Inquiry" is missing, and that's
where the work is.

| Stage | What they're thinking | What has to exist | What actually moves them |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Unaware | "This is just how it is." | Nothing | Not your buyer yet. Ignore. |
| 1. Triggered | "This can't keep happening." | Nothing yet | An event, not a message. See §1. |
| 2. Nagging | "We waste so much time on this." | LinkedIn posts naming the problem | Recognition. Describing their situation better than they can. |
| 3. Looking | "Is there something for this?" | Search pages, Google Business Profile, an industry piece | Proof of judgment |
| 4. Interested | "Could this person help us?" | §01, prior work, About with a face | Feeling understood before contact |
| 5. **Ready-ish** | "What does this cost and what's the first step?" | **Price floors, the map, the sample map** | **A small first step that isn't a five-figure decision** |
| 6. Inquiry | "Sent it. Hope they're real." | Intake form, fast reply | Reply speed. Under one business day, always. |
| 7. Fit call | "Do they get it?" | 25 minutes, structured | You asking better questions than they expected |
| 8. The map | "This is already useful." | The map engagement | The artifact itself |
| 9. Proposal | "Which version do we do?" | Three options, fixed prices, care plan included | Choosing scope, not choosing whether |
| 10. Build | "Is this on track?" | Working software every two weeks | Visible progress, not status reports |
| 11. Handover | "Do we understand what we own?" | The documentation plate | Ownership without dependency |
| 12. Care plan | "Who do we call?" | Retainer, offered in the proposal | Continuity |
| 13. Advocate | "You should talk to my guy." | A scheduled referral ask | Being asked, specifically |

**Stage 5 is the gap in both documents.** Between reading the site and emailing
a stranger about a five-figure project there was nothing. The free map fills it.
That is its main job, ahead of qualification.

**Stage 1 is the gap nobody has noticed.** People at stage 1 convert fastest and
nothing in the plan reaches them, because they aren't searching yet. Only
outbound and referral reach stage 1. That's the argument for §6.

## 5. The pipeline

Eight stages. Track it in a spreadsheet. At this volume a CRM is a solved problem
you don't have.

| Stage | Exit criteria | Target elapsed |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Inquiry | Intake received | n/a |
| 2. Qualified | Real problem, budget band stated, decision-maker identified | 1 day |
| 3. Fit call held | 25 min. Problem, budget, timeline, authority confirmed. | 5 days |
| 4. Map booked | Ninety minutes scheduled, one workflow agreed | 10 days |
| 5. Map delivered | Diagram, friction inventory, ranked list handed over | 17 days |
| 6. Proposal sent | Three options, fixed prices, care plan as a line item | 21 days |
| 7. Won or lost | Signed, deposit paid | 35 days |
| 8. Delivered | Launched, documented, care plan active | +6 to 12 weeks |

**Planning assumptions, not data.** Replace these with real numbers after ten
inquiries and do not treat them as forecasts before then: inquiry → qualified
60%, qualified → fit call 70%, fit call → map 50% (higher than it would be if
paid), map → proposal 80%, proposal → won 50%. That last pair is the entire
point of the structure. By the time someone has watched you understand their
business for free, the build is the obvious next step rather than a decision.

**The only metric that matters in year one:** *qualified conversations with
owners per month.* Target eight. At 6 to 12 deals a year, funnel percentages are
statistical noise; conversation count is not. Everything else in the plan is a
means to that number.

### Disqualify fast, and kindly

- Budget under $10k. "No figure yet" is fine on a first contact and is not a
  disqualifier; refusing to discuss it on the fit call is.
- Wants a marketing website
- Has an app idea but no business and no money
- Wants a technical cofounder, or offers equity instead of cash
- "Just send me a quote" with no willingness to talk
- Decision-maker won't join a call
- Wants to own the process but not participate in it

A warm "not a fit, here's who I'd call instead" costs one email. In a market
this small, that email circulates.

### The referral ask, scheduled

Day 30 and day 90 after launch. Put it in the calendar at kickoff. Not "let me
know if you know anyone," which gets nothing. Instead:

> "Who else do you know running six systems and reconciling them by hand on
> Fridays?"

Specific asks get answers.

## 6. The outbound motion: "I made you a map"

This is the highest-return move available to you, and neither document has it.

You have a named market of a few thousand firms and a deliverable that costs you
three hours. So:

1. Build the list. 200 businesses, by name, from the segments in §1. Owner or
   GM name against each. Public sources: business registrations, permit filings,
   LinkedIn, industry associations, Chamber directories.
2. Watch for triggers. Second location, new ops hire, new GM, funding, a bad
   review that describes an operational failure.
3. Pick one. Spend two to three hours building a **speculative partial map** from
   what's publicly observable: their booking flow, their intake form, their
   customer-facing steps, whatever their reviews complain about.
4. Send it. Not a pitch. A thing.

> "I map how work moves through Hawaiʻi businesses. I did a rough one for yours
> from the outside, mostly the customer-facing half, because your booking flow
> does something I see a lot. It's attached. It's free and there's nothing
> attached to it. If the inside looks different from what I guessed, I'd be
> interested to know where."

Why this outperforms everything else available to you:

- It reaches stage 1 buyers, which no other channel does
- It is impossible to ignore and awkward to be rude to
- It demonstrates the differentiator instead of claiming it
- It's the "If I Owned…" series pointed at one real prospect, so it doubles as
  content
- Ten a month is thirty hours, and ten of these will beat a year of SEO

**Do not publish these.** Your brand guide's version of the series says research
five to ten real named businesses and map their friction publicly. In a market
where everyone knows everyone, publishing "here's what's broken about how this
company operates" is a bad trade. Send it privately to the business itself.
Publish only composites, and say in the piece that it's a composite.

## 7. Where I'd change the earlier strategy

Three disagreements with `STRATEGY.md`, for the record:

**1. Drop the App Cost Calculator.** It's ranked as page two there. I'd cut it.
It commoditises you, invites price comparison against firms competing on price,
and any honest output is a range so wide it's useless. It also directly
contradicts the positioning, which is that figuring out what you need *is* the
product. Replace it with a **friction self-assessment**: twelve questions, a
scored result, and a one-page summary of where time is going. Same email
capture, aligned with the positioning, and its output leads straight into the
map instead of into a price negotiation.

**2. Give away three maps, not one.** That document suggests one free map for a
publishable case study. Three anonymised published maps plus a downloadable
sample are the assets you're actually blocked on. This is now moot since you've
made the map free, but the point stands: publishing them is the value, and it
should be a deliberate goal rather than a by-product.

**3. Answer the bus-factor question on the site.** Not mentioned there. Every
solo five-figure software deal has this objection sitting underneath it and it
is almost never voiced. Named contractor bench, code ownership, documented
handover. Copy is drafted in `HOMEPAGE-COPY.md` §06.

Otherwise I agree with it, including the things it parks: industry pages until
there's evidence, YouTube as backlog, Instagram as a mirror of LinkedIn, and no
productisation until you've built the same thing four times.

stop.

`/custom-software-hawaii`.

First "If I Owned…" composite. `/automation-ai-hawaii`. The friction
self-assessment. Second round of outbound, now with three published maps behind
it.

**Month 4 to 5**
Read Search Console, count qualified conversations, find which stage leaks, and
replace the assumptions in §5 with real numbers.
